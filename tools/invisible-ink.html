Invisible Ink
Why

Your model reads characters your screen refuses to draw.

Paste a RAG chunk. Watch the hidden order appear.

Unicode has a block of 128 tag characters, U+E0000–U+E007F, that map one-to-one onto ASCII and render as nothing. UTS #51 puts it plainly: a tag-unaware renderer "will display any sequence of tag characters as invisible, without any effect on adjacent characters." Your tokenizer is not tag-unaware. So a support ticket, a scraped page, a PDF or a retrieved chunk can carry a full paragraph of instructions that no human reviewer will ever see. This page decodes them, shows you the sentence, and hands back a clean string.

What breaks

  • Stripping is lossy on purpose. ZWJ (U+200D) and ZWNJ hold together emoji sequences and are required in Persian, Hindi and other scripts. Strip them globally and you corrupt legitimate text. The toggle is separated from the tag-block toggle for exactly this reason — leave it off for user-facing content.
  • Flag emoji use the tag block legitimately. Subdivision flags are U+1F3F4 + tag letters + U+E007F per UTS #51 Annex C. This scanner reports those separately and does not count them as an injection, but a blanket regex strip elsewhere in your pipeline would break them.
  • Sanitizing is not a defense on its own. It closes the invisible channel. Plain visible text saying "ignore previous instructions" is still an injection, and OWASP's LLM01 guidance asks for privilege separation and capability minimization, not just input filtering. The phrase detector here is a hint, not a shield.
  • Homoglyph mapping can mangle real multilingual text. A Russian word is not an attack. The mapper only fires on mixed-script runs inside otherwise-Latin words, and it stays off by default.
  • NFKC normalization rewrites more than you expect — ligatures, full-width forms, superscripts. Fine for retrieval keys, risky if you echo the text back to a user.
