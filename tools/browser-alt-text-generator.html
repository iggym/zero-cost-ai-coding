Why this exists

Missing alt text is one of the most common accessibility failures on the web — WebAIM's 2026 audit of a million homepages found alt text missing or unusable on roughly a quarter of images with alt text present at all, and over half of homepages have at least one image with none. It's usually not neglect on purpose — writing alt text for a big batch of images is tedious, and hosted captioning APIs charge per call or gate the good models behind a paid tier. This runs the model locally instead.

The tool

Free tier: none needed. The model (Xenova/vit-gpt2-image-captioning, ~240 MB, quantized) downloads once from the Hugging Face Hub CDN and is cached by your browser — after that, captioning is unlimited, offline-capable, and costs nothing per image.

What it replaces

Hosted image-captioning APIs (Google Cloud Vision, Azure Computer Vision, various "alt text generator" SaaS tools) typically charge per 1,000 images once you're past a small monthly free allotment, plus they require an account and a card on file for the paid tier. Running the model client-side removes both the per-call cost and the account.

How to use it

  1. Click the drop zone and pick one or more images, or drag them in. Nothing uploads anywhere — the files stay in your browser tab.
  2. On the first image, the model downloads (a few seconds to a minute or two depending on your connection). Watch the progress bar.
  3. Each image gets a generated caption in an editable text box — clean it up if the model got something wrong.
  4. Copy a single caption with the per-image copy button, or use copy all as HTML alt="" attributes to get a ready-to-paste block for every image.

What breaks

  • Model quality is modest. vit-gpt2 gives short, generic captions ("a dog sitting on a bench") — good as a first draft, not a substitute for someone writing alt text that reflects the image's actual purpose on the page.
  • No text-in-image reading. It won't transcribe text baked into an image (screenshots, memes, diagrams with labels) — it describes scenes, not text.
  • First load needs internet. The ~240 MB model has to download once from Hugging Face's CDN; after that it's cached and works offline, but a flaky connection can make that first load fail or stall — reload and retry.
  • Memory-hungry on old phones. Running a vision-language model in WebAssembly is heavier than typing in a text box — expect it to be slow or to fail on low-memory mobile devices. A laptop or desktop browser works best.
  • No batching parallelism. Images process one at a time in sequence, so a folder of 200 photos will take a while — this is a captioning aid, not a bulk pipeline.
